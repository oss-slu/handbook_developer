# 1. Introduction and Core Principles

## About This Handbook

This handbook is your orientation guide to working as a developer in Open Source with SLU. It covers practices and expectations that apply across all projects in the program. It is not an exhaustive reference. It gives you enough structure to get started and enough context to make good decisions when situations aren't covered explicitly.

Your team will define its own norms through a Working Agreement. Your Tech Lead will provide project-specific guidance. This handbook is the shared foundation underneath both.

## The Program

Open Source with SLU projects are real-world, long-lived software systems developed collaboratively by student teams, faculty mentors, and external contributors. Each project may differ in domain and technology stack, but all share a commitment to quality engineering, open collaboration, and long-term sustainability.

The software you build will be used by real people and maintained by future teams. That context shapes everything in this handbook. The practices here aren't academic exercises. They're the same practices used by professional software teams, adapted for a program where learning and delivery happen together.

## Your Role as a Software Developer

As a developer, you are responsible for more than writing code. You are responsible for:

- **Delivering working software.** Code that solves real problems, passes tests, and can be maintained by the next team.
- **Communicating clearly.** With your teammates, your Tech Lead, and your client. In Slack, in GitHub, in meetings, and in your code itself.
- **Supporting your teammates.** Through code reviews, pair programming, knowledge sharing, and honest feedback.
- **Thinking beyond the current sprint.** Your work has consequences beyond this week. Consider the developer who will read your code next semester.

## Guiding Principles

These principles inform the practices throughout the handbook. They're not rules to memorize. They're lenses that help you make good decisions when the handbook doesn't cover your specific situation.

### Developer Empathy

Developers are people. Respect different skill levels, learning curves, and life constraints. The developer who is struggling with a new framework this week might be the one who helps you debug a tricky issue next week. Build the kind of team environment where people can grow.

### Context is King

Always consider the project's goals, constraints, and users before proposing solutions. A technically elegant approach that doesn't fit the project's architecture, timeline, or team capabilities is not the right approach. Understand the local reality before reaching for a general answer.

### Collaboration First

Prefer shared understanding over individual optimization. Software built by a team that communicates well will always outperform software built by isolated individuals, no matter how talented. When in doubt, over-communicate. Document decisions. Share what you've learned.

### Maintainability First

Write code that others can understand, test, and extend. The clever solution that only you can maintain is a liability, not an asset. Favor clarity over cleverness, consistency over novelty, and simplicity over sophistication.

### Excellence

Strive for professionalism in code, communication, and conduct. Excellence is not about being perfect. It's about caring enough to do your best work, asking for help when you need it, and continuously improving. It shows up in the quality of your commits, the thoughtfulness of your code reviews, and the way you treat your teammates.

## Time Management and Sustainability

Software development is a marathon, not a sprint (despite our use of the word "sprint"). Sustainable pace matters more than occasional heroics.

- **Communicate early when you're overwhelmed.** Your Tech Lead can help rescope work or redistribute tasks, but only if they know there's a problem.
- **Break tasks into manageable pieces.** A large issue is less intimidating and easier to estimate when decomposed into smaller steps.
- **Ask for help before deadlines, not after.** Reaching out on Tuesday gives your team options. Reaching out on deadline day gives them almost none.
- **Respect rest as part of productivity.** Burnout makes you less effective, not more. Consistent, sustainable effort produces better results than late-night scrambles.
