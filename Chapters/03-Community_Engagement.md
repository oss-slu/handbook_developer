# 3. Community Engagement

Your project is open source software. That means it exists within a broader community of users, contributors, and maintainers. Community engagement is not an extra activity layered on top of your development work. It's part of how open source software succeeds.

## Open Source Culture

Open source is built on transparency, shared ownership, and the idea that software improves when people can see it, use it, and contribute to it. Working in the open means your code, your issues, your PR discussions, and your documentation are all visible. This is a feature, not a constraint. Visibility creates accountability, invites collaboration, and builds trust.

A few cultural norms are worth internalizing early:

- **Work in the open.** Discuss decisions in GitHub issues and PRs where they're discoverable. Avoid making important decisions in private channels where others can't find or reference them.
- **Lower the barrier.** Make it easy for someone new to understand your project, set it up, and contribute. This means good documentation, clear issue descriptions, and labeled "good first issues."
- **Give credit.** Acknowledge contributions from others. Thank reviewers. Recognize when a teammate's suggestion improved your approach. Credit is free and builds goodwill.

## Contributing to Your Project

You are both a contributor to and a maintainer of your project. That dual role gives you useful perspective.

As a contributor, follow the same practices you'd expect from an external contributor: read the contributing guidelines, write clear PR descriptions, and respond to feedback. These habits keep the project healthy and model good practices for anyone who might contribute from outside the team.

As a maintainer, you're responsible for the project's quality and accessibility. Keep the README current. Write issues that someone outside your team could understand. Respond to external contributions (if they arrive) promptly and respectfully.

## Issue Hygiene

Issues are the public record of your project's work. Keep them clean and useful.

- **Write clear descriptions.** An issue should explain what needs to happen and why, with enough context that someone unfamiliar with the codebase can understand it. See the issue templates in the [Appendices](../Appendices/Templates.md) for structure.
- **Use labels consistently.** Labels like `bug`, `enhancement`, `documentation`, and `good first issue` help people find relevant work quickly.
- **Close what's resolved.** When an issue is fixed or no longer relevant, close it with a brief note explaining the resolution. Don't let stale issues accumulate.
- **Provide reproduction steps for bugs.** A bug report without steps to reproduce is difficult to act on. Include what you did, what you expected, and what actually happened.

## Responding to External Feedback

If your project receives issues, questions, or contributions from people outside your team, respond professionally.

**Assume positive intent.** External contributors are volunteering their time. Even if a contribution doesn't meet your project's standards, the person behind it is trying to help. Acknowledge their effort before explaining what needs to change.

**Respond promptly.** An unanswered issue or PR creates a poor impression of the project. Even a brief "Thanks for this, we'll review it this week" is better than silence.

**Escalate when needed.** If you receive feedback that you're unsure how to handle, whether it's a complex technical question, a request that conflicts with the project's direction, or inappropriate behavior, bring it to your Tech Lead or program staff.

## Code Ownership and Maintenance

In open source, ownership means responsibility, not control. You are a steward of the codebase for the time you're on the team. Your job is to leave it in better shape than you found it so the next team can pick up where you left off.

This means:

- Writing code and documentation that don't depend on your presence to be understood.
- Following established project patterns rather than introducing personal preferences that fragment the codebase.
- Planning for handoff from the start, not as an afterthought at the end of the semester.

Your project will continue after your iteration ends. The care you put into maintainability, documentation, and clean code is how you serve the developers who come after you.

## Open Source Standards

Every project in the program should meet baseline open source community standards:

- **License file.** An OSI-approved license that defines how the software can be used and distributed.
- **README.** Project overview, setup instructions, and links to documentation.
- **Contributing guidelines.** How to report bugs, suggest features, and submit code.
- **Code of conduct.** Expected behavior for participants in the project community.
- **Security policy.** A `SECURITY.md` describing how to report vulnerabilities. The program maintains a standard security policy that all projects should include.

If your project is missing any of these, raising it with your Tech Lead is a valuable contribution in itself.
