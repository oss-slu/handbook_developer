# 7. Program Requirements

This chapter describes what the program expects of you as a developer and how your contributions are assessed. The specifics of deadlines and submissions are published in your course materials. This chapter covers the underlying expectations and assessment dimensions so you understand what you're working toward.

## Contribution Expectations

### The Baseline: One Merged PR Per Sprint

At minimum, you are expected to complete one full PR cycle per sprint. That means taking ownership of an issue, raising a functional PR by the Week 1 deadline, responding to review feedback, and getting your PR merged by sprint end. This demonstrates that you can participate in a professional development workflow.

Consistently delivering one merged PR per sprint represents baseline participation. It shows competence, but not distinction.

### Beyond Baseline

One PR per sprint is the floor, not the ceiling. Stronger contributions look like:

- **Advancing Milestones.** Your team has iteration Milestones (see [Chapter 6](06-Project_Workflow.md)). Work that meaningfully advances these larger goals demonstrates that you understand the bigger picture, not just individual tickets.
- **High-impact contributions.** Some work matters more than others. Fixing a critical bug, implementing a key feature for a client demo, or unblocking a teammate creates outsized value. Look for high-impact opportunities, not just easy wins.
- **Sustained momentum.** Multiple PRs across sprints, when appropriate, builds trust and demonstrates reliability. Sustained effort is more valuable than occasional heroics.
- **Craftsmanship.** Well-documented code, thoughtful commit messages, proactive testing, and clean PR descriptions show that you care about your work and respect your teammates' time.

## Shared Ownership

Every developer shares responsibility for the project's success. This means more than completing your assigned issues. It means caring about the codebase as a whole, noticing when documentation is out of date, raising concerns when you see a problem, and supporting your teammates when they're stuck.

Shared ownership also means that the team's outcomes reflect on everyone. If the project is behind, the entire team should be looking for ways to help, not just the person assigned to the struggling feature.

## Assessment Dimensions

Your performance is assessed holistically across five dimensions. These mirror how professional software developers are evaluated in industry.

### Discovery

Your engagement with understanding the problem space. This includes participating in client meetings, conducting research, exploring solution approaches, and asking thoughtful questions. Discovery is how you ensure that what you build actually solves the right problem.

### Delivery

The actual work you ship. Merged pull requests, implemented features, resolved issues, and documented solutions. Delivery demonstrates your ability to execute plans, write maintainable code, and produce tangible results. Quality matters as much as quantity.

### Collaboration

Your effectiveness as a teammate. This includes code reviews, communication quality, support for teammates, and contribution to a positive working environment. Providing constructive feedback, responding to reviews professionally, and helping create a team where everyone can do their best work all contribute here.

### Demonstration

Your ability to showcase and explain your work. Sprint demos, presentations, and the clarity with which you communicate about your contributions. Can you explain what you built and why it matters to someone who wasn't in the code with you?

### Client Satisfaction

How well the team serves its client. This reflects whether the team delivers value, incorporates feedback appropriately, and maintains professional relationships. Client satisfaction is a team outcome, but individual contributions to client communication and responsiveness are noted.

## Exception Reports

When you deviate from an expected norm, whether that's a missed deadline or a deliberate process adaptation, you owe a written account. This is called an exception report.

An exception report is not a punishment. It's a professional communication practice. It makes your reasoning visible so it can be evaluated in context. A well-documented exception demonstrates professional maturity. Silence about a missed commitment does the opposite.

Exception reports should include: what varied and when, the context and circumstances, evidence (commit history, Slack messages, work logs), your reasoning, and what you'll do differently going forward. Submit proactively when possible. A report filed before the deadline passes carries more weight than one filed after the fact.

For deadline misses, be concrete about what will change. "I'll start earlier" is not a plan. "I'll raise a draft PR by Thursday even if the implementation is incomplete, so my Tech Lead has visibility" is a plan.

For process adaptations, explain your reasoning and what you learned. Thoughtful adaptation based on your team's context is valued. Skipping steps without reflection is not.

## Professional Development Activities

The program includes workshops, readings, and reflective exercises designed to support your growth as a professional software developer. Engage with these genuinely. The developers who get the most out of the program are the ones who treat these activities as opportunities for growth, not checkboxes to complete.
